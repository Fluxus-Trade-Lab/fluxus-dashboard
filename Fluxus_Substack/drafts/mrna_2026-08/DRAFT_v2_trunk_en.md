Those are the facts. What follows is the review.

And the review has exactly one point: **in the twelve days before that headline, nothing I did right had anything to do with it.**

**Three things you can take away from this:** how sector relative strength decides *which one* · how to turn "is the structure tight enough" into a number · how to make position size a division problem instead of a decision.

As for the news — it showed up on day five, and I still can't tell you why that day.

### ① Why not semis

The first half of August was a grind. On the 13th and 14th what I was actually watching was $MU — **because I owned semis, and they weren't working.** On the 17th it ran 4% and I briefly felt clever. On the 18th it gave back 7%.

The group data had been saying the same thing for two weeks: **semis never printed a positive number all month** (three-month excess between −0.10 and −0.19), while **software was the strongest group in the market.** My book was already sitting in it.

**On any given Thursday there are four hundred setups that look valid. Something has to decide which one you're allowed to spend risk on.**

For me that's sector relative strength — and it earns its keep **when the index is weak.** When everything's ripping, outperformance tells you nothing. When the index is sliding and one group is still beating it, that spread is real.

**Those numbers are trustworthy because no human writes them.** Institutions are forced by their own risk mandates to sell weakness and chase strength; they don't get a vote. **Sector relative strength isn't sentiment. It's the footprint of machines that don't have any.**

**Tonight: put your holdings' sector next to the index, same window. The one that's behind — why are you still in it?**

### ② The pool, and the one that surfaced

To qualify for my pool a stock needs three things: two million shares a day, price above the 50-day, three-month relative strength in the top 20%. **That's the liquid leader definition — not mine, and it only tells you which water to fish. It says nothing about when to drop the line.**

**When to drop the line is my own rule. I call it *surfacing* — the second a fish breaks the surface, you can't yet tell whether it came up for air or is about to jump. Those two look identical.** So the scanner doesn't judge; it hands me whatever surfaced today. All five, same day:

1. A 20-day high
2. A 4% up day in there
3. Price above the 20-day
4. Three-month RS over 80
5. **RS self-percentile near 100** — today is its strongest day in twenty

That last one is the only one that matters. **Relative strength isn't "went up a lot," it's "went up more than the market"** — a stock that falls less in a down tape has rising RS. And the **self-percentile asks where today ranks against itself: sort its last twenty RS readings, see where today lands. That's the whole calculation.** A stock climbing off the floor might have an RS level of 60, nowhere near the leaderboard, **but if its self-percentile is 100, today is the strongest day it has had in twenty. The rankings just haven't caught up.**

**I bought intraday on the 14th; the scanner listed it that night. The machine confirmed me. It didn't tell me.**

Whether it was a fish or something else — **I found out on the 19th.**

**Copy all five. Don't skip the fifth.**

### ③ The chart

The structure has a name: **launchpad**. Every moving average compresses into one narrow band under the price, which means every timeframe's holders now sit at roughly the same cost. Once price is above all of them, everyone underneath is waiting to be lifted.

And compression is visible, which means it's measurable — take the highest of the 10/20/50-day averages, subtract the lowest, divide by price. **$MRNA's band went from 25% to 4.1% in six weeks. Six times tighter.** Not "looks tight." Tight *relative to itself*, which is the only comparison worth making.

My rule is short: **no tightness, no trade.**

And $60 is the nail the whole thing hangs on. Prior resistance, a small handover, then one large green bar straight through it. **Above 60 this is a trade. Below 60 it isn't one.** The same level works twice because the people who traded there last time still remember their cost:

> **The chart has no memory. The people who bought at that price do.**

**Where the target came from**: the COVID run took it from the twenties to 120 in a few months with almost no trading in between — a fast zone nobody accumulated in. Empty on the way up, empty on the way back. **The target was 120, and so was the speed.**

Why it chose the 19th, nobody can say. **All I could tell you was that 60 was the line.**

**Your own chart has a line like that. Find it and write it down — that's your stop, not whatever you think you can stomach.**

## How much

**I bought twice on Aug 14. Both positions were 5% of the account. One cost thirteen times more than the other.**

| | Structural entry | The VWAP add |
|---|---|---|
| Stop | **60**, under the line | **20 cents** under VWAP |
| Risk per share | **$2.72** · 4.34% | **$0.20** · 0.32% |
| Size | **5%** | **5%** |
| **Account risk** | **0.217%** | **0.016%** |

The first buys the **structure**, so the stop has to sit under the line that would prove the structure wrong. No negotiating. The second buys **order in that moment** — above VWAP, with the day — and if it's wrong I know in minutes. Same 5%, almost free.

> **Position size is not risk. Stop distance is risk.**

It's a division problem, not a decision:

> **Size = risk budget ÷ stop distance**

On the 14th: 0.25% ÷ 4.34% ≈ 5%. **I didn't decide on 5%. I arrived at it.**

**The stop is close because the structure is tight, not because I'm timid.** The reverse is the useful part: **a messy chart with nowhere to put a stop mechanically gets a tiny position. I don't have to summon discipline to pass on bad structure. The arithmetic passes for me.**

## The times I made it smaller — and the one time I made it bigger

**Aug 14.** My own regime read said full size, normal adds. I put on 5%.

**Aug 18.** Three sessions of deterioration — 63% of the market was above its 20-day the day I bought, under half three days later. And the position itself had done nothing for three days. **My losers get three days. That rule had come due.** I sold the add back out at my entry price.

**Two rules fired at once. Neither of them knew a readout was coming the next morning.**

**Aug 19, pre-market.** Price came through 100, I saw the first selling into it, and I sold half — **still with no idea what had happened.** Then I did something entirely unromantic: I asked an AI what the news was. While it worked, I pulled the long-term chart and found 120 and 126. **Before I knew a single thing about oncology, I knew where I'd meet sellers.**

The answer came back: the biggest news this company has had since COVID. **So I bought it back, pre-market**, when the averages caught up and it pushed through again. By the open I had 10% on at a lower average cost than I started with.

> **When you don't know what happened, take the risk off. When you know, put it back on. The unit of decision is size, not opinion.**

**The first minute of the open, RVOL exploded** — not big volume, volume far beyond its own usual first minute. Above the one-minute opening range, buyers have the floor; below it, sellers do. **That entry belonged to anyone who wasn't already in.** For me, the same bars were the referee for selling on the way up.

**Ninety minutes after the open**, flat. The trade was still technically valid when I left — **which is exactly when to leave.** It touched nearly 200 in the extended session that afternoon. I was asleep.

Every one of those decisions cost me money in hindsight.

**I'd make them all again.** The version of me that skips them is also the version that doesn't survive the trades where the news goes the other way. **I held that position overnight, and gaps have two directions.**

**Write the rules while you're calm. Because the day one comes due is the day you won't want to follow it.**

## The account

**R is what a trade is allowed to cost me, fixed before I click. It's a set percentage of account value — 0.25% for me.**

**It turns dollars into method.** In that division, the money never enters the calculation. From then on every decision happens inside the process, not inside the money.

**It turns method into pain.** 0.25% isn't a risk parameter. It's a pain parameter — how much this one trade is permitted to hurt. **I set it while I was calm, so by the time the frightened version of me sits down, there are no buttons left to press.**

**Once the pain is bounded, you stop needing more signal.** Most people hunt for confirmation not because confirmation works, but because they never drew a line around the uncertainty — so the only thing left to buy is a little more certainty. **Bound the pain and you don't need it.**

After that I count in R, not dollars. **Dollars tell you how big my account is, which is none of your business and no use to you. R tells you the shape of the method, which is the part you can take.**
