Nothing I did right in those twelve days had anything to do with the news, and you will leave with the three things that did: sector strength picks which one, tightness filters the trade, and risk determines the size.

I never ask will it go up. I ask who is being bought, and then I hunt the next big elephant trade with tight risk.

① Sector — why not semis

Through the first half of August the answer to who is being bought was not semis, which I owned and kept owning while they went nowhere. $MU ran 4% on the 17th and I felt smart for exactly one day; on the 18th it took every cent of it back. The group hadn't printed a green number all month while software had the best excess in the market — and my book was already there in $PLTR, $NOW, $ZETA and $RBRK, because that rotation happened weeks earlier.

Sector RS isn't sentiment — it's the footprint of Institutional algo monkeys, and mine had been walking out for two weeks while I watched.

On any Thursday four hundred setups look valid. Sector strength earns its keep when the index is weak — when everything is going up, outperformance means nothing.

Put your sector next to the index tonight and ask why you still hold the one that's behind.

② The pool, and the one that surfaced

Qualifying is three numbers — 2M shares a day, above the 50-day, three-month RS top 20%. That's the liquid leader definition, not mine, and it only says which water to fish.

When to fish is my rule. I call it surfacing — a fish breaks the surface and you can't tell if it came up for air or is about to jump. The scanner doesn't judge, it hands me whatever surfaced today. All five, same day:

  1. A 20-day high
  2. A 4% up day in there
  3. Above the 20-day
  4. Three-month RS over 80
  5. RS self-percentile near 100 — today is its strongest day in twenty

The fifth one is the scanner.

RS isn't "went up a lot," it's "beat the market" — a stock that falls less on a red day has rising RS, which is why it turns green while the chart still looks bad. The self-percentile compares it to nobody but itself: sort its last twenty readings, see where today lands. A name crawling off the floor might sit at 60, nowhere near anybody's leaderboard, but self-percentile 100 means its character just changed and the rankings haven't noticed. That gap is where I get paid.

I bought intraday on the 14th and the scanner listed it that night. The machine confirmed me, it didn't tell me.

The timing standard I took from Jeff Sun's $KC breakdown — the list hands you the name before it moves, not after. What I didn't take is his holding period: he sits in a name for a month, my winners average ten days.

③ The chart

Every average compressed into one band under price — two-week money, two-month money, ten-month money all at the same cost. Once price is above them, everyone underneath is carrying you.

Highest of the 10/20/50 minus the lowest, over price. $MRNA went 25% to 4.1% in six weeks, six times tighter against itself.

My rule is four words: no tightness, no trade.

Then there's $60 — old resistance, a handover, one big green bar through it. Above 60 it's a trade, below 60 it's nothing. The same level works twice for an unmystical reason:

The chart has no memory. The people who bought at that price do.

Where the target came from: COVID took this from the twenties to 120 with almost nobody trading on the way, so nobody in there needs convincing to let price back through. Empty up, empty back. Target 120, and quickly, or I'm wrong.

Why the 19th? Nobody knows. What I knew was that 60 was the line, and I'd watched this shape resolve the same way too many times to ignore.

How much

Two buys on Aug 14, same stock, same 5% — one cost thirteen times the other.

Trade 1, the structure: in 62.72, stop 60, $2.72 a share, account risk 0.217%.

Trade 2, the VWAP add: stop 20 cents under VWAP, account risk 0.016%.

Same size, thirteen times the cost, and the difference was never conviction:

Position size is not risk. Stop distance is risk.

Size = risk budget ÷ stop distance

0.25% ÷ 4.34% ≈ 5%. I didn't decide on 5%, I arrived at it. The stop sits 0.73 ATR from entry — close because the structure is tight, not because I'm brave. I am not brave. And it runs in reverse too: a sloppy chart with nowhere to put a stop gets a tiny position automatically. I never need discipline to pass on garbage. The arithmetic passes for me.

What I cut, and the once I added

Aug 18. Three sessions of decay — 63% of the market above its 20-day when I bought, under half by then — and the position had done nothing for three days. My losers get three days. The rule came due and I paid it, selling the add at entry.

Two rules fired at once and neither knew a readout was twelve hours out. That's not a flaw in the rules, that's what rules are.

Aug 19, pre-market. I trade from Asia, so the first leg happened while I was at breakfast. Price through 100, first selling into it, sold half — still with no idea what happened — then did the least romantic thing in trading and asked an AI what the news was.

While it worked I pulled the long chart. Above 100 only history is left: 120 is old support turned resistance, the anchor off the all-time high lands at 126. Before I knew anything about oncology, I knew where the sellers lived.

Biggest news this company's had since COVID. So I bought twice the size I'd just sold, pre-market, and by the open I had 10% on at a lower cost than I started.

When you don't know what happened, take risk off. When you know, put it back on. You decide in size, not in opinions.

First minute of the open, RVOL exploded — not big volume, volume its own first minute has never seen. Above the one-minute opening range the buyers own the floor. That open was a real entry for whoever wasn't in yet. For me the same bars were the referee for selling into it.

Ninety minutes in, flat. The trade was still technically perfect when I left, which is exactly when to leave. It touched nearly 200 that afternoon. I was asleep.

Every cut cost me money in hindsight and I'd make every one again — the version of me that skips them doesn't survive the story where the readout goes the other way. Gaps don't check which direction you were hoping for.

The account

R is what a trade is allowed to cost me, fixed before I click: 0.25%. Not more when I really like it. There's no really like it line in the formula.

R turns dollars into method — the money never enters the division, so every decision happens inside the process instead of inside the money. And R turns method into a pain unit: 0.25% is how much this one trade is allowed to hurt. I set it while calm, so when the frightened version of me sits down there's nothing left for him to press.

Once the pain is bounded you stop needing more signal. People hunt confirmation because they never drew a line around the uncertainty, so the only thing left to buy is the feeling of being sure. That feeling costs a fortune and is worth nothing.
