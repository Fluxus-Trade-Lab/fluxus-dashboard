## I don't have opinions. I have measurements.

I measure three things before I click: where the money is rotating, how tight the chart is, and what being wrong will cost me.

**I never ask "will it go up." I only hunt for the next big elephant trade with tight risk.**

## Three filters, in order

Sector first, then what surfaced, then the chart.

**Sector strength is not a step, it is a map, and I redraw it every day.** So when $MRNA showed up I did not need to go research biotech, I already knew where biotech stood. The homework was finished before I needed it, which is the only time homework is worth anything — setups do not wait for you to catch up.

### ① Why not semis

The first half of August the market was grinding, and what I was actually watching was $MU, **because I owned semis and they were not working.** On the 17th it ran 4% and I briefly felt clever, on the 18th it gave everything back.

〔📷 2 ▶ QQQ 日线〕

The group data had been saying the same thing for two weeks: **semis never printed a single positive number all month, while software was the strongest group in the entire market** — and my book was already sitting in software, because that rotation happened weeks earlier.

〔📷 3 ▶ 板块三线图〕

〔📷 4 ▶ XBI × SNOW 并排〕

On any Thursday there are four hundred setups that look valid, and something has to decide which one gets your risk. For me that is sector relative strength, and it earns its keep exactly when the index is weak — when everything is going up, outperformance tells you nothing, but when the index is bleeding and one group refuses to bleed with it, that spread is real.

**Sector RS isn't sentiment — it's the footprint of institutional algo monkeys.** They are forced by their own risk rules to dump weakness and chase strength, and they never get a vote.

Try it tonight: put your holdings' sector next to the index over the same window, and ask yourself why you are still holding the one that is behind. I could not answer that question for $MU, and that was the answer.

### ② The pool, and the one that surfaced

To qualify for my pool a stock needs three things — two million shares a day, price above the 50-day, three-month RS in the top 20%. **That is the liquid leader definition, which is not mine, and it only tells you which water to fish. It says nothing about when.**

When is my own rule. I call it **surfacing**: the second a fish breaks the surface you cannot tell whether it came up for air or is about to jump, they look identical, so the scanner does not judge — it just hands me whatever broke the surface today. All five, same day:

1. A 20-day high
2. A 4% up day in there
3. Above the 20-day
4. Three-month RS over 80
5. **RS self-percentile near 100** — today is its strongest day in twenty

The fifth is the whole scanner, the first four are qualifiers.

**Relative strength is not "went up a lot," it is "beat the market"** — a stock that falls less on a red day has rising RS, which is why it turns green while the chart still looks terrible. And the self-percentile compares the stock to nobody but itself: sort its own last twenty readings and see where today lands, that is the entire calculation. A name crawling off the floor might have an RS of 60, nowhere near any leaderboard, **but a self-percentile of 100 means its character just changed and the rankings have not noticed yet. That gap is where I get paid.**

$MRNA's eight sessions before I bought:

〔📷 5 ▶ RS 折线 + 80 门槛〕

One thing belongs on the record: **I bought intraday on the 14th and the scanner listed it that night. The machine confirmed me, it did not tell me.**

*The timing standard I took from Jeff Sun's $KC breakdown — the list has to hand you the name before it moves, not after. What I did not take is his holding period: he can sit in a name for a month, my winners average ten days. Same logic, different people, different rules.*

Copy all five, and do not skip the fifth.

### ③ The chart

The structure is called a **launchpad**: every moving average compressed into one narrow band under the price, which means every timeframe's holders — the two-week money, the two-month money, the ten-month money — are all sitting at roughly the same cost, and once price is above all of them, everyone underneath is carrying you up.

Compression is visible, which means it is measurable: highest of the 10/20/50 minus the lowest, divided by price. **$MRNA went from 25% to 4.1% in six weeks — six times tighter against itself, which is the only comparison that counts.**

〔📷 6 ▶ 束宽五柱〕

My rule is four words: **no tightness, no trade.**

Then there is $60, the nail the whole thing hangs on. Old resistance, a small handover, then one large green bar straight through it. **Above 60 this is a trade, below 60 it is nothing.** The same level works twice for a very unmystical reason:

> **The chart has no memory. The people who bought at that price do.**

**Where the target came from:** the COVID run took this stock from the twenties to 120 in a few months with almost nobody trading on the way, a fast zone — nobody accumulated in there, so nobody needs convincing to let price back through. **Empty on the way up, empty on the way back. The target was 120, and it had to get there quickly, or I was wrong.**

**Your chart has a line like 60 on it too. Find it and write it down — that is your stop, not whatever you have decided you can emotionally survive.**

## How much

Almost nobody publishes this part, which is strange, because it is the only part that is fully yours.

**I bought twice on Aug 14, same day, same stock, same 5% — and one position cost thirteen times the other.**

**Trade 1, the structure:** in 62.72, stop 60, $2.72 a share, account risk **0.217%**.
**Trade 2, the VWAP add:** stop 20 cents under VWAP, account risk **0.016%**.

〔📷 10 ▶ 13.6× 对比〕

Same size, thirteen times the cost, and the difference was never conviction:

> **Position size is not risk. Stop distance is risk.**
>
> **Size = risk budget ÷ stop distance**

On the 14th that was 0.25% ÷ 4.34% ≈ 5%. **I did not decide on 5%, I arrived at it.** The stop sits close because the structure is tight, not because I am brave — and the same arithmetic runs in reverse: **a sloppy chart with nowhere to put a stop gets a tiny position automatically, so I never need discipline to pass on garbage. The arithmetic passes for me.**

## The times I made it smaller, and the once I made it bigger

**Aug 14.** My own regime read said full size, normal adds. I put on five.

**Aug 18.** Three straight sessions of decay — 63% of the market was above its 20-day when I bought, and now it was under half — and the position itself had done nothing for three days. **My losers get three days. The rule came due and I paid it**, selling the add back at my entry.

〔📷 11 ▶ 宽度三柱〕

**Two rules fired at once, and neither of them knew a phase III readout was twelve hours away.** That is not a flaw in the rules. That is what rules are.

**Aug 19, pre-market.** I trade from Asia, so the whole first leg happened while I was at breakfast. I came back to the desk, price was through 100 with the first wave of selling into it, and I sold half — **still with no idea what had happened.** Then I did the least romantic thing in trading: I asked an AI what the news was.

While it worked I pulled the long-term chart. Above 100 we were through the 52-week high, so only history was left: **120 was old support turned resistance, and the anchor off the all-time high landed at 126. Before I knew one thing about oncology, I knew where the sellers lived.**

The answer came back — the biggest news this company has had since COVID. **So I bought it back pre-market, when the averages caught up and price pushed through again**, and by the open I had 10% on at a lower cost than I started with.

> **When you do not know what happened, take the risk off. When you know, put it back on. The unit of decision is size, not opinion.**

**The first minute of the open, RVOL exploded** — not big volume, but volume far beyond its own usual first minute. Above the one-minute opening range the buyers own the floor. **That open was a real entry for whoever was not in yet.** For me the same bars were the referee for selling on the way up.

〔📷 12 ▶ 一分钟图〕

**Ninety minutes after the open I was flat.** The trade was still technically perfect when I left, which is exactly when to leave. It touched nearly 200 in the extended session that afternoon, and I was asleep.

Every one of those cuts cost me money in hindsight, and **I would make every one of them again** — the version of me that skips them is the same version that does not survive the story where the readout goes the other way. I held this position overnight, and **gaps do not check which direction you were hoping for.**

**Write your rules while you are calm, because the day one comes due is the day you will hate it.**

## The account

The unit first, because the unit is the product.

**R is what a trade is allowed to cost me, fixed before I click: 0.25% of the account.** Not more when I really like it — there is no "really like it" line in the formula.

**R turns dollars into method.** The money never enters the division, so every decision after that happens inside the process instead of inside the money.

**And R turns method into pain.** 0.25% is not really a risk parameter, it is a pain parameter — how much this one trade is allowed to hurt. **I set that number while I was calm, so when the frightened version of me finally sits down at the desk, there is nothing left for him to press.**

**Once the pain is bounded you stop needing more signal.** People hunt for confirmation not because confirmation works but because they never drew a line around the uncertainty, so the only thing left to buy is feeling a little more sure. Bound the pain and you will not miss the feeling.

**0.25% is what I paid to be in the room when the luck walked in.**
