〔标题槽 · 工作标题：**Breadth is the worst since the dot-com era. I'm still long semis. Here's where I'd stop.**〕

The median stock is sitting about 16% below its one-year high. @ConnorJBates_ called it the weakest reading since the dot-com bubble. The same week, the semiconductor ETF made a new high.

Both of those are true. Over the past two weeks I've argued both sides of this in my own Discord — once to dismiss the breadth worry, once to say it's exactly the thing that could end the trade. This is the version where I put the two sides next to each other, and then write down what would make me change my mind. As far as I can tell, nobody arguing about breadth on X has done that last part.

## How bad it is

Here are four readings from the October 1 release of our dashboard:

| | |
|---|---|
| Share of stocks above their 50-day average | **25.21%** |
| Share above their 200-day | **36.41%** |
| New 52-week highs vs new lows (common stock only) | **7 vs 29** |
| McClellan oscillator — NYSE | **−31.21** |
| McClellan oscillator — Nasdaq-100 | **+36.67** |

Look at the last two rows. Same ruler, two rooms. The broad market reads like a selloff. The Nasdaq-100 reads like a rally.

That split is the whole debate. On X, people posted one ruler each — one dashboard reading "thrust," another reading "negative" on the same day — and nobody put them side by side. Put them side by side and the argument stops being about whether breadth is bad. It is. The argument becomes *where* it's bad, and why.

## Why it's bad: the train hit the brakes

Think of liquidity — the speed at which money is being printed and pushed into markets — as the speed of a train.

This year the train didn't stop. Somebody hit the brakes: the Fed hiking on one side, the Treasury propping up the bond market on the other. If the braking is clean, the train keeps going, just slower than before. If it's clumsy, the ride gets bumpy. Right now the market's path is the sum of two forces — the momentum it already had, plus the reaction force of the brakes — and every disagreement you're reading comes out of that sum.

The brakes don't hit every car the same way. Rate hikes made bad breadth because the companies that don't make much money now make even less. That is what IWM is. In a higher-for-longer regime, what holds up is cash flow plus growth — and that's a short list of sectors.

J Law (@JLawStock) put the arithmetic behind this on October 2. With the 10-year around 5%, a Treasury's P/E-equivalent is roughly 18–20 — about the same as the S&P 500's forward P/E, which FactSet had at 19.2 on September 25. When a low-risk asset pays that, a stock has to earn its place with real earnings growth, cash flow and staying power. Smaller companies with thin margins and no pricing power can't. His conclusion: if rates stay higher for longer while earnings growth stays concentrated in a handful of sectors, weak breadth could persist.

It is concentrated. FactSet's estimates for third-quarter earnings growth, year over year, as of September 25:

| | Q3 2026 est. earnings growth |
|---|---|
| S&P 500 | **+29.1%** |
| Energy | +111.4% |
| Information Technology | **+63.5%** |
| Industrials | +14.9% |
| Utilities | +6.8% |
| Health Care | +5.9% |
| Financials | **+3.3%** |
| Consumer Discretionary | +3.0% |
| Consumer Staples | **+2.9%** |

The index is growing earnings at almost 30%. More than half the sectors in it are growing at single digits.

@ConnorJBates_ put the other half of this into numbers on September 30: the breadth damage is concentrated in IWM, RSP, XLU, ITB and XLF. Inside semis, software and cybersecurity, breadth isn't bad at all.

So the question isn't "is breadth bad." It's whether the car I'm sitting in is one of the ones still being pulled.

## Why I'm still long semis: the chart

Semis made a new high on September 22. When they lead, they really lead.

What I watch isn't the index, it's the rotation underneath it. Over the last month the money has moved from one sub-theme to the next — CPUs first (AMD, ARM, INTC), then memory (MU, SNDK), then photonics, then equipment (LRCX, KLAC, AMAT on September 29), and on October 1, the small semis: TSEM, FORM and SMTC broke out to new highs. A group that rotates internally can keep going a long time. It's also why the easiest way to hold it is SOXX itself.

On the pullbacks, the anchored VWAP from SOXX's all-time high has acted as support. MU wasn't extended on ATR from its 50-day when I looked on September 22. And on the September 28 gap-down, I could see put sellers in semis stepping in — they usually put in the low of the day.

## Why I'm still long semis: the business

Higher-for-longer rewards companies that make cash, and these make a lot of it.

CPUs are getting re-rated: ARM, AMD and INTC are both beneficiaries and bottlenecks of the agentic-AI build-out, and OpenAI and Meta are big customers. Foundries have the deepest moat of anything in AI, and TSMC and Intel are already running out of capacity. MU's quarter on September 30 was a massive print. NVDA announced a 150-billion buyback on September 28.

The same FactSet report has the number I'd lead with. Semiconductors and semiconductor equipment are expected to grow earnings 126% this quarter. Take semis out, and the whole technology sector's growth drops from 63.5% to 24.2%. The tech trade is, to a large extent, the semis trade.

Semis and AI are still in their up-phase. They're still the first choice.

## The case against me

I'll start with my own words, from October 1:

> "Narrow leadership is normally fine for a while, but if everything else continues to worsen, it means everyone is hiding in those leaders. At one point there will be no buyers left, and macro will crash it too."

That's the bear case, and I don't have a rebuttal to it. I have a condition (below).

Other people have made it sharper. @Signal_Sigma pointed out that only twice in the past two years has breadth been worse than now — March–April 2025 and March 2026 — and he couldn't find a historical analog for SPY sitting this close to its high with breadth this bad. @thesetupfactory wrote that the RSP/QQQ ratio is at an extreme: the rotation should come, and if it doesn't, *that's* when to worry, because it means money is leaving stocks altogether. @cfromhertz noted tech is now about 39.5% of the S&P 500, above the March 2000 record.

The same 1999 chart makes their point too. When the Nasdaq-100 topped in March 2000, the advance-decline line was already near its low. Breadth was right in the end. It was about eight months early.

It wasn't a one-off. In 2007 the NYSE advance-decline line peaked at the beginning of June. The NYSE Composite kept making new highs in July and again in October, and the S&P 500 topped on October 9 — about four months after breadth did. In late 2021, as Lance Roberts noted, the line stalled nearly six months before the correction that took hold in January 2022.

## The case for ignoring it (for now)

Also my own words, September 28:

> "Bad breadth can continue while money piles into narrow leadership. Look at 1999."

![NYSE advance-decline line vs Nasdaq-100, January 1999 – July 2000 (StockCharts)](1999_nyse_adline_vs_ndx.jpg)

The NYSE advance-decline line rolled over in the summer of 1999 and fell almost without a break into March 2000. Over those same months the Nasdaq-100 roughly doubled, from about 2,300 to about 4,700.

And from the same post: crowded does not mean reversal. In a secular bull market, macro concerns sometimes stay only concerns.

The same goes for yields. The worry right now is a 10-year near 5%. In the 1990s, 5% was the floor.

![In the 1990s bull market, the 10-year closed at or above 5% on 95.5% of trading days](1990s_10y_above_5pct_fluxus.png)

From its October 1990 low to its March 2000 peak, the S&P 500 rose 417%. Across those 2,366 trading days, the 10-year Treasury closed at or above 5% on 95.5% of them. It held above 5% on every single close for 1,979 days in a row, until LTCM and Russia pushed it under in September 1998. The median close for the whole decade was 6.4%.

J Law made the general version of this argument. Backtests can build a strong bearish case: high-yield periods have often come with major drawdowns, and adding weak breadth and index levels makes the case look even more convincing. But rain makes the ground wet, and wet ground doesn't necessarily mean it just rained. "Historical relationships matter, but they are not laws of physics." Change one important variable — earnings, valuations or liquidity — and the same setup can produce a very different outcome. For him, the variable that's different now is earnings.

@ArtofSpecuycky made the same comparison independently — money squeezing into a handful of high-return giants the way it did in the 1999 rate-hike cycle. @RealSimpleAriel noted the share of stocks above the 50-day in his universe had dropped below 30%, the weakest since March, and that last time breadth stayed weak for weeks before it turned. Weak can stay weak. In the same post, he said he had more actionable names than at any point since April.
