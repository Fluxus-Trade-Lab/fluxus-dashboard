# Play the reaction, not the outcome

In March, the Strait of Hormuz closed for the first time in history, and I spent most of that month not knowing what was going to happen next. That turned out to be fine.

Here is the note I sent my members on March 2nd, before any of it resolved:

> *"We are not trying to predict the outcome of the war, rather only playing the reaction of the market."*

That one sentence did more work for me this half than any macro call I could have made. But "don't predict" is useless advice on its own — it's the thing people say right before they go ahead and predict. So here is what it actually looked like, five ways.

## 1. Turn the event into a table, not an opinion

March 23rd, the night before we found out what Iran would do with the strait. This is verbatim:

> 1. He bombs the plant → Iran hits back → we dump −100/−150 ES, maybe −200.
> 2. NATO opens the strait without bombing → +100 ES.
> 3. Iran opens it but taxes everyone except the US → +150 ES.
> 4. Opens to everyone, US included, with a fee → +200 ES.

Four branches. Each one priced. I had a lean — I said I was vibing with 3 or 4 — but the lean isn't the product. **The table is.** Because whichever branch printed, I already knew what the tape was worth, which meant I already knew what I could risk.

An opinion tells you who to root for. A table tells you how much to bet.

## 2. Find one instrument that prices the whole story

I stopped reading war headlines in March and started watching crude.

> *"As long as crude is trending up, rates are up, equity is not going to be easy for upside."* — March 27
> *"We need to see crude below 95 and falling to signal a market reversal. **Crude is the truth detector here.**"* — March 30

Crude was pricing two things at once: the war, and the rates. So one number replaced an entire news feed. That's not a shortcut, it's the opposite of a shortcut — it's the recognition that a market has already read the news faster than I can, and priced it into something I can put on a chart.

Every macro mess has one of these. Find it, and you get to stop refreshing.

## 3. Hedge so that both branches pay

The protection I used in April was bought in January, while everything felt fine:

> *"I will look into index puts 2–3 months out… SPY 650p 4/17."* — January 30

But the better one was this:

> *"My hedge for Iran escalation is XLE calls. So, win-win situation."* — February 19

If the war escalated, energy went up and the calls paid. If it de-escalated, my equities went up and the calls were the cost of sleeping. **That isn't insurance, it's a position that wins on both branches** — and the difference between those two things is most of what people get wrong about hedging.

One more, from March 12, and this one is a reversal:

> *"Put protections are at the historic extreme, so only waiting till a proper situation improvement."*

When fear gets expensive enough, the price of protection stops being protection and starts being a signal. I stopped buying it and started waiting for the improvement instead.

## 4. Decide what you're allowed to ignore

This is the part nobody writes down, and it might be the most valuable.

> *"Macro events like the Japan bond situation will take care of themselves while we should worry less about them. This is still AI vs Macro."* — May 18

And on whether the new Fed chair would hike, in the middle of everyone having a very confident opinion about it:

> *"**Nobody knows**, but I tend to believe Warsh will talk tough to establish his hawkish credibility. He wants to appear tough versus blowing up the system. Hard to see him hike right in the face of a sell-off."* — July 28

Notice what that is. It isn't a rate forecast. It's a read on what a person in that chair is *incentivized* to do. I can't forecast rates. I can read a motive, and I can size for being wrong about it.

## 5. And if you're going to be awake anyway, watch Korea

I trade US markets from Japan, which means I watch the money move while America sleeps. The most useful thing I found this year:

> *"KOSPI down 10% today, second worst day in 25 years. **The Korean index is a leveraged product of QQQ.**"* — June 23

Korean retail runs the same trade as US momentum, with more leverage and less patience. So when Seoul cracks, it usually cracks first. I've been using it as a forward gauge since June, and it has been early every time.

## What would change my mind

- Crude reverses back above its recent range and holds → the truth detector says risk is back on the table, and I go back to smaller size regardless of what the index does
- The protection bid gets expensive again → stop hedging, start waiting
- Korea stops leading — if KOSPI holds while US momentum breaks, the tell is broken and I need a new one

---

*P.S. — Everything above is a way of admitting I don't know what happens next, dressed up with enough structure that I can still act. That's really all a process is. The people who look calm during a war aren't calm because they figured out the war. They're calm because they wrote down what they'd do in each case before they had to do it.*
