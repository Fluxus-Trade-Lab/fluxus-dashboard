I spent years consuming everything I could from the traders who had already walked this path.
Dan Zanger / Oliver Kell / William O'Neil / Mark Minervini / Stan Weinstein / Kristjan Qullamaggie

O'Neil's work on the CAN SLIM system gave me structure. Minervini taught me how to think about setups and timing. Weinstein's stage analysis became the lens through which I view every chart. But the real shift came when I found traders like Kristjan Kullamägi, better known as Qullamaggie.
His approach to risk management, position sizing, etc completely changed how I thought about building a position. Oliver Kell, Shake Pryzby, Sean... these guys reinforced the importance of process, discipline, and trading your own psychology instead of trying to trade like someone else.
I mention these names not to name drop, but because it's important to acknowledge that no one does this alone.
Every single concept I use, every rule I follow, came from someone who was generous enough to share what they learned the hard way. I studied their interviews, their old posts, their chart reviews, etc.
I went back in time through thousands of charts, looking at volatility contractions, explosive pivots, how setups formed and failed. I wanted to see the patterns for myself, not just read about them.
...and that's the work that matters.
There are no shortcuts, and if you're not willing to put in the hours studying history, you're going to keep making the same mistakes everyone else makes.

── Core Mindset ──
At some point, I realized I was overcomplicating everything.
I had indicators stacked on indicators, multiple timeframes open, scanning for every possible edge. I thought more information would give me more control.
It didn't. It just made me slower and more confused.
So I stripped it all down, and now I focus on three things: math, risk/reward, and price.
Price is the only thing that's real, and everything else is just noise.
Shake Pryzby has a phrase that stuck with me: "Perfect entries only." It sounds extreme, but it's not about being flawless... It's about being selective. It's about waiting for the setup to come to you instead of forcing trades because you're bored or because you feel like you need to be doing something.
Trading is a math game, not a prediction game.
I'm not trying to guess what the market will do next. I'm waiting for the market to show me what it's doing, and then I'll decide if the math makes sense for me to risk capital.
If it doesn't, I don't trade, and yes... it's that simple.
and I'll be the first to say that I'm not a genius. I don't have some innate ability to read charts better than anyone else. I understand my strengths and my weaknesses, and I trade accordingly.
My strength is discipline and my weakness is impatience. So I built rules around my impatience to make sure it doesn't destroy me. I know I can't outsmart the market, so I don't try.
I focus on drawdown control, keeping my losses small, and on surviving long enough to catch the moves that actually matter!!
There are no shortcuts to this you physically have to go back in time and look through thousands of charts. You have to analyze previous cycles, study volatility contractions, understand how explosive pivots form, and figure out how you can trade around these setups in a way that fits your psychology.
because if you skip that work, you're just gambling.

── Psychology ──
Self-awareness is the real edge.
I'll say it again because it's the most important thing I've learned in four years: self-awareness is the real edge.
There are two types of traders:
The boom-and-bust traders who swing big, make huge gains, then blow up and have to start over.
And the risk-averse traders who grind slowly, compound steadily, and survive.
I used to be the first type, and currently I'm trying to become the second.
That shift only happened because I started journaling every single trade and reviewing it at the end of the day. It's very uncomfortable to have a living mirror in front of you at all times.
You have to sit with your mistakes, and you have to admit when you broke your rules or let emotion override your plan.
But that discomfort is what forces accountability and helps you separate emotion from process!! You have to know your own tendencies.
I know I get impatient when the market is slow.
I know I overtrade when I'm on a winning streak because I start to feel invincible.
These are my patterns and they show up over and over again in my journal, and because I can see them, I can build rules to counteract them.
I never try to predict where the market is going. I follow price, so if price is moving up and the setup is there, I'm a buyer. If price is breaking down and the setup is failing, I'm out. It doesn't matter what I think should happen, because what matters is what is happening right now!!
"You overestimate what you can do in a year and underestimate what you can do in ten." I think about that constantly now.
I used to think I could master trading in twelve months if I just worked hard enough, which looking back, is laughable. This game takes years... but if you think long-term, if you focus on staying in the game instead of getting rich fast, then ten years from now you'll look back and realize you've built something real.
Also, I'm not an academic weapon. I'm just someone who respects risk and focuses obsessively on drawdown control. In trading, you can lose everything in one trade. Not because the market is out to get you, but because one really bad decision (holding through a catastrophic loss, ignoring your stop, overleveraging into a failing trade) can wipe you out.
Jesse Livermore said it best: "The market doesn't beat them, they beat themselves." and that's the truth.
The market just does what it does, and we're the ones who destroy ourselves by refusing to accept when we're wrong.
The best losers win (that's the paradox of trading).
There's a natural human desire for certainty, for control, for being right, so as traders we have to surrender that. You have to accept that you will lose, often, and that your job is not to avoid losses but to keep them small and manageable.
The biggest thing (the only thing that actually matters in the long run) is keeping your drawdown small. If you can do that, you give yourself enough time and enough capital to be around when the real opportunities show up.
...and that's the whole game.

── My Trading Style ──
I'm an aggressive momentum swing trader and trend follower, focusing on trading weekly breakouts and 3-5 day movers.
My entire system revolves around the idea of tension and release: when a stock compresses into a tight range with low volume, it builds energy for a breakout and expansion.
I'm looking for episodic pivots: earnings reactions, gap ups or downs, Stage 2 transitions, flat bases, pullbacks, and all time high breakouts. These are the setups where the risk/reward is defined and the potential for quick bursts of momentum is highest.
I risk 1-3% of my portfolio per trade, and that number varies based on equity curve feedback and current market conditions. If I'm in a drawdown, I scale back. If I'm performing well and the market is cooperating, I can afford to be more aggressive.
My stops are tight, and my win rate is low, which is intentional because I'm not trying to be right most of the time. I'm trying to keep my losers small and let my winners run!! The only losers I accept are small ones... anything else is a violation of my rules.
I focus on fast moving stocks in hot sectors, anything >3% ADR.
Slow, grinding stocks don't fit my system, because in order for me to make money, I need volatility and I need the price to move decisively so I can get in, manage the position, and get out with a clear read on whether the setup is working or not.

── Timeframes ──
I watch multiple time frames to understand context and timing:
5, 15, 30, and 65 minute charts for intraday structure/execution.
daily, weekly, and monthly charts for the bigger picture/trade ideas.
The higher timeframes tell me where I am in the trend, while the lower timeframes help me refine my entry and manage the trade in real time.

── Indicators ──
I keep it stupid simple.
I use the 9/21 and 50EMA, plus the 10WEEK moving average.
These help me identify trend direction and support levels.
I rely heavily on Jeff Sun's Swing Data indicator (top right on my charts) to track important data and identify the best place to get positioned on the chart.
Simple Volume with Pocket Pivots helps me see where the buyers and sellers are positioned on the chart.
I also use ATR% multiples from the 50 MA, so when a stock is more than 8 ATR away from the 50, it's extended, and I'm cautious.
Anchored VWAP and Fibonacci retracements round out the toolkit.
They're just frameworks that help me see what price is doing and where the probabilities are in my favor.

── Setup ──
My setup is built around finding entries with the highest potential risk multiples.
I'm looking for 9 EMA pullbacks, weekly bases, Stage 2 breakouts, all time high breakouts, anything that shows tight price action, coiling, or consolidation.
The key word is TIGHTNESS.
If there's no tightness, there's no trade.
A stock that's chopping around in a wide range or drifting aimlessly doesn't offer the kind of setup I need. I want compression and I want the chart to look like it's coiled and ready to explode.
That's where the best risk/reward is.

── Entry Settings ──
First thing to note is that entry tactics are a form of risk management.
I'm not just trying to get in... I'm trying to get in at a point where my risk is defined and my probability of follow through is maximized.
I focus on 3-5 day movers because that's the timeframe where my system works best.
My ideal entry tactics include: inside days / opening range highs / undercut and reclaims / VWAP reclaims
These are all areas of tightness where my risk can be defined.
I'm watching prior day high and low for breakouts or undercuts, opening range highs on the 5, 15, or 30 minute charts depending on market environment, and new intraday range highs.
I don't enter if the stock is more than 60% above the distance from the low of the day, because for me that's too extended intraday. Also, I rarely enter if a stock is more than 4 ATR away from the 50 MA. Anything beyond that is too stretched, and the odds of a pullback or reversal increase significantly.
I use the 30 min pivot to find aggressive swing entries on weekly breakouts. This lets me get in earlier with a tighter stop while still respecting the higher timeframe setup.
...and I keep my watchlist tight, only 3-5 names at the open.
More than that and I lose focus... I'd rather watch a few setups closely than try to track ten half baked ideas!!

── Risk Management ──
Everything I do revolves around drawdown control, because if I can keep my drawdowns small, I survive long enough to compound when the market cooperates.
My entire approach is built on risk/reward math, I'm constantly looking at my average R/R and win rate to understand if my system is working in the current environment or breaking down. I'm typically risking around 1% of my account per trade depending on the environment.
When the system is working and I'm catching runners, I increase size and push harder!!
When the system weakens and I'm getting chopped up or stopped out more than usual, I pull back immediately. Less risk, more caution, smaller positions.
I don't trade consistently aggressive all the time. I trade in bursts of performance and adapt my risk accordingly.
If the market is giving me clean setups and follow through, I lean in.
If it's not, I step back and wait.
This isn't about being heroic or forcing trades when conditions aren't there. It's about recognizing when the environment matches my edge and when it doesn't.
something I do that helps me stay detached emotionally is I hide my P&L intraday.
I don't want to see the dollar swings while I'm managing positions. It messes with my head... If I see a big profit, I'm tempted to lock it in early. If I see a loss growing, I might hold longer than I should hoping it comes back. Hiding it forces me to focus on price action and my rules instead of the money.
It's a small psychological trick, but it works for me!!

── Stop Settings ──
Everything I do I try to make as simple as possible.
99% of the time I keep my stops at the low of the entry day.
Stocks tend not to revisit the low of the breakout day if the setup is actually working. If price comes back down and takes out that level, it's telling me the breakout failed and it might need more time still.
There's no reason to hold through that. I don't give it room to "work itself out" or hope it recovers. If the low of the day gets violated, I'm out.
Clean stop, clean exit, move on to the next idea.

── Sell Rules (Discretionary) ──
Selling into Strength:
I usually scale out of winners in 20-25% increments of my initial position depending on the current environment.
When a trade hits 2R, I ALWAYS take off a piece and move my initial position to breakeven. That locks in some profit and removes the risk of turning a winner into a loser.
As the stock continues to move and breaks through my next target level, I take off another piece. If price gets extended from the daily 9 EMA and I'm starting to feel euphoric (usually right around the time I'm tempted to take a screenshot for Webull) that's my signal to trim again.
I've noticed over the years that Euphoria is a warning sign for me.
When I feel like the trade can't possibly go wrong, that's exactly when I need to be taking something off the table. Selling into strength is how I lock in gains and avoid giving back profits when the move inevitably pulls back.

Selling into Weakness:
It's important to note that when using a trailing stop I'm usually selling manually, I rarely use market orders because I want control over my fills (important with options). If the stock closes below a key moving average like the 9 or 21 EMA, that's a signal the momentum is breaking down and I need to get out.
I also exit if price closes below a swing low, intraday low, or bar low that I'm using as my risk point. These levels represent structure to me, so if it breaks I'm out. When those pivots break, the setup is compromised.
I don't wait around to see if it recovers... I cut it and reassess.
Selling into weakness is about protecting capital and keeping my losses small. If the trade isn't working, there's no reason to hold onto it!!

Beyond the mechanics, I'm always asking myself two questions:
What stage is the market in, and what stage is my equity curve in?
If the stock is just starting a move, I can afford to hold longer and give it room. If it's near previous highs or extended from the EMAs, I'm more aggressive about taking profits because the edge isn't clear.
The same logic applies to my equity curve.
If I'm in a drawdown, I tighten up... smaller size, quicker exits, more caution. If I'm making progress and my curve is trending up, I can lean in. If my equity curve is at all time highs, I revisit my process to make sure I'm not getting sloppy or overconfident.
The market changes, and so does my performance, so I have to adapt to both!!

── Market Conditions & Sector Analysis ──
Most of my profits are made during short, concentrated periods of time.
This past fall I had a crazy run and took advantage of a two month period where everything I bought worked immediately for me.
...and that's the reality of momentum trading it comes and goes.
The market doesn't cooperate all the time, and knowing when to play aggressive versus when to step away is critical. I'm constantly watching where the major indices are relative to the 9 and 21 EMAs. If we're above those levels and holding, the environment supports my setups. If we're chopping below them or breaking down, I start to pull back my chips.
I also watch how prior leaders are behaving, are they holding major support pivots and breaking to new highs, or are they rolling over and failing?
The length of my watchlist tells me something too.
If I can only find three decent setups, that's a sign the market isn't giving me much to work with. If I have ten "high quality names" showing tightness and setting up, that's a different story.
Different traders have their own methodology and favorable market conditions. What works for me might not work for someone else, and that's fine.
I focus on my own trading world, the conditions where my edge shows up, the setups that fit my psychology, and the timeframes that match my system. I don't try to trade every environment or chase every move.
I wait for my spots, and when they show up, I push hard.
When they're not there, I step away and preserve capital. That discipline is what separates surviving in any market environment.

── Stage Analysis ──
I'm obsessed with Stage 1 bases transitioning into Stage 2 breakouts.
This is where the biggest moves start.
A stock that's been consolidating for weeks or months, building a tight base, and then finally breaking out with conviction... that's the setup I want to be in.
Once a stock is in Stage 2, I buy every clean pullback and flag. These aren't reversals or failed setups. They're healthy pauses within a larger uptrend, and they offer some of the best risk/reward entries you can find.
The stock has already proven it can move so now it's just a matter of timing the re-entry as it digests gains and sets up for the next leg!!

── ETF Scanning ──
I use ETF scanning to identify which sectors are setting up before individual stocks break out.
I'm looking for ETFs that are forming Stage 1 bases and look ready to move into Stage 2.
Once I find a sector that's coiling and about to expand, I hunt for the leaders within that ETF, for example the stocks with the tightest bases, the healthiest volume patterns, the strongest relative strength.
If the sector is moving, I want to be in the names that are leading the charge, not lagging behind. The ETF gives me the macro view and the individual stocks give me the execution!!

── Beach Ball Analogy: Relative Strength ──
I think about strong stocks like beach balls being held underwater.
The longer you hold them down, the harder they pop up when you finally let go and that's relative strength.
A stock that refuses to break down during a market pullback, that holds its key levels while everything else is bleeding, that's storing energy.
When the market turns back up, those are the names that explode first and move the hardest. I'm not interested in stocks that fall apart every time the market corrects.
I want the ones that show resilience, the ones that are fighting to go higher even when conditions aren't perfect. So think of this analogy in the future!!

── Sector Weighting ──
There's an old saying: "a stock is 40% the market, 30% the sector, and 30% itself."
I think about this constantly.
You can be looking at the "cleanest chart" in the world, but if the overall market is in a downtrend and the sector is weak, that stock is fighting an uphill battle.
Stock selection is everything.
You can have the best trading skills, the tightest risk management, the most disciplined execution... but if you're in the wrong names or the wrong sectors, none of it matters.
I'd rather be average at execution in a leading stock within a hot sector than perfect at execution in a lagging name that's going nowhere. The stock does most of the work.
My job is to pick the right ones and focus on protecting my downside.

── How to determine Market Conditions ──
One of the biggest mistakes I see beginner traders make is getting lost in the noise. There's so much data, news, analysis, and opinion floating around, and everyone's style is different. But the reality is that every trader, no matter their approach, has one universal source of truth.. their own P&L.
Your equity curve is your feedback loop.
For me, understanding market conditions isn't about memorizing every headline or obsessing over macro predictions. It's about ranking the factors that give me actionable context and then letting them guide my aggressiveness, my sizing, and my positioning.
Here's how I think about it, in order of priority:

1) Your Equity Curve: The Ultimate Feedback.
I always start with myself.
How are my trades performing? How is my risk/reward playing out?
Your P&L tells you whether your approach is working in the current environment.
If the indices are weak but your setups are working, trust yourself. Stay in your positions, focus on your entries, and let your system do the work.
If the market looks strong but you're in a losing streak, ignore others commentary on the market. Get defensive, cut risk, and recalibrate.
For me, this is the #1 determinant of how aggressive I want to be. My own results guide me more than anyone else's opinion.

2) Scanning & Watchlists: Hot Names and Themes.
Next, I look at the actions of individual stocks and my watchlists.
How long are my watchlists?
short WL = weaker environment
long WL = many opportunities = strong environment
Which names are holding up? Which sectors or themes are rotating in/out?
Watchlist upgrades and downgrades matter because they show me how healthy the market is right now.

3) Indices & Market Structure: Broad Context.
Finally, I check the overall market and I keep it simple as can be while only looking at structure and moving averages:
9 EMA > 21 EMA > 50 EMA on $SPY & $QQQ give me a quick read on overall trend health.
Above EMAs = Good Environment
Below EMAs = Bad Environment
Breaks of structure or moving averages provide potential inflection points and areas to be more defensive.
I never make the market the sole driver of my trades, but it's useful as context for how aggressive I want to be with position sizing.
